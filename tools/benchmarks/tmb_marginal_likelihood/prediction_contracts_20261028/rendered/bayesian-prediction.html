A prediction for a surveyed site reuses information about that site’s effects. A prediction for an unsurveyed site must also allow its unknown effects to vary. The same distinction applies to years. This guide shows how to make those choices explicitly, retain dependence among observations in the same year, and distinguish expected responses from future observations.

Start with vignette("bayesian-scales", package = "multiScaleR") for fitting and interpreting annual-scale models. The examples here use actual stored NUTS fits to artificial data. Reading them requires no optional sampling software. Long fitting calls are shown but do not run during a package build.

1 Choose the prediction you need

Question Function Variation included
What is the scale for these covariates or years? predict_scales() Joint scale-parameter uncertainty; new-year variation when requested
What is the expected response? predict() Joint parameter uncertainty and requested group effects
What observations might I record? simulate() The above plus observation sampling variation

For binomial models, predict(type = "response") returns success probabilities. For Poisson and negative-binomial models it returns expected counts, and for Gaussian models it returns expected responses. type = "link" returns logits for binomial models, log means for count models, and means for Gaussian models. An interval for an expected response does not include observation sampling variation. Use simulate() when that variation belongs in your question. Each prediction draw is an expected response conditional on that draw’s parameters and included group effects. Its posterior median need not equal the median of future observations. The mean and sd columns are finite Monte Carlo summaries, not proof that the corresponding theoretical moments exist. Prefer medians and quantiles for heavy-tailed new-group predictions.

2 Predict another survey at known sites and years

known_fit <- annual$fits$pooled
surveys <- annual$data[1:3, ]
surveys$count <- NULL  # Future responses are not inputs to prediction.

known <- predict(known_fit, surveys, kernel_inputs = annual$inputs,

These are expected-response summaries from joint posterior vectors. Observation noise is excluded. The contract records that distinction without changing the prediction or deciding whether the fit is reliable. Read the fit’s original diagnostics separately. simulate() records posterior predictive replicates, which include observation noise. Predictions that sample an unseen year’s or site’s effect identify the added group variation; setting an effect to zero remains a reference calculation. See ?prediction_contract for the fields.

Prepared native predictions use the same descriptions in snapshot$prediction_contract, with one entry per prepared output type. Their native standard errors and confidence intervals condition on selected spatial scales. They do not acquire the population-parameter uncertainty of Bayesian predictions. Raw Bayesian draw matrices and cached native numerical outputs retain their original structure and attributes.

kernel_inputs supplies prepared bins for the prediction locations and maps. Each unique prepared location has a unique binned$point_ids entry. The row_id column matches observations to those entries. Observation rows can be reordered or repeated, including repeated visits to one location. row_id defaults to the fitted ID-column name when the fit retained it; supplying it explicitly also works with older saved fits.

For your own maps, use kernel_prep() or kernel_prep_by_group() as described in vignette("year-specific-rasters", package = "multiScaleR"). Keep the fitted kernel, predictor definitions, and projected map units. New inputs may use a different numerical distance normalizer, but must express the same physical units. Prediction retains the training center, spread, contrasts, and formula bases. Do not standardize the new rows independently.

3 Predict the scale distribution in an unobserved year

The annual-scale model uses a population log scale and annual deviations:

\[ \log D_y = a + \tau z_y, \qquad z_y \sim N(0,1). \]

Here, \(D_y\) is a distance in map units and \(\tau\) is the between-year SD of log distances. For the Gaussian kernel, \(D\) is sigma; for the exponential kernel, it is the decay distance. It is not the finite preparation cutoff or a radius containing a specified fraction of kernel weight. Both \(a\) and \(\tau\) remain uncertain. For an unobserved year, new_levels = "sample" samples its deviation using each complete posterior parameter vector. It includes uncertainty in \(\tau\) rather than plugging in one estimate.

future_years <- data.frame(year = c("2026", "2027"))
future_scale <- predict_scales(known_fit, future_years,
  new_levels = "sample", seed = 801)
knitr::kable(future_scale[c("group", "distribution", "median", "lower", "upper",
  "p_below_min_D", "p_above_max_D")], digits = 3,
  col.names = c("Year", "Distribution", "Median", "Lower", "Upper", "Below range", "Above range"))
Year Distribution Median Lower Upper Below range Above range
year=2026 new_group_predictive 165.058 36.813 503.959 0.200 0.001
year=2027 new_group_predictive 163.198 37.772 488.931 0.215 0.001

How to read the output: new_group_predictive identifies a distribution for an unobserved year’s scale. Its interval includes variation among years and uncertainty about the population parameters. It does not estimate that future year’s realized scale as if its responses had been observed. The exchangeable model gives 2026 and 2027 the same distribution before observing either year. Small differences between their printed summaries are Monte Carlo variation, not an estimated trend. When future responses become available, refit with those observations to estimate that year’s realized effects. Prediction alone does not update the posterior with new responses.

The two support columns report probability below and above the fitted reference range. They do not assess coverage of an unspecified future map. Bayesian distance priors have unbounded support; these probabilities are important when substantial mass falls outside the distances your maps resolve. Use medians and quantiles for strongly skewed distance distributions. A finite Monte Carlo arithmetic mean does not establish that its theoretical mean exists. Use scale = "log" to summarize natural log distances for extreme tails.

3.1 Understand the zero-deviation reference

reference_scale <- predict_scales(known_fit, future_years,
  new_levels = "zero")
knitr::kable(reference_scale[c("group", "distribution", "median", "lower", "upper")],
  digits = 2)
group distribution median lower upper
year=2026 zero_deviation_reference 162.85 51.24 408.47
year=2027 zero_deviation_reference 162.85 51.24 408.47

This sets the new year’s \(z_y\) to zero. The reference retains uncertainty in \(a\) but omits future-year variation. It is not the average distance over years: even conditional on fixed \(a\) and \(\tau\), the mean of \(D_y\) is \(\exp(a + \tau^2/2)\), whereas the zero-deviation distance is \(\exp(a)\). The fitted posterior dependence between \(a\) and \(\tau\) matters when averaging over their uncertainty.

local({
  x <- rbind(future_scale[1, ], reference_scale[1, ])
  op <- par(mar = c(4.5, 9, 1, 1)); on.exit(par(op))
  plot(x$median, 2:1, xlim = range(c(x$lower, x$upper)), ylim = c(.5, 2.5),
    yaxt = "n", ylab = "", xlab = "Landscape scale D (meters)", pch = 19)
  segments(x$lower, 2:1, x$upper, 2:1)
  axis(2, 2:1, c("Unobserved year", "Zero deviation"), las = 1)
})
New-year scale prediction and the zero-deviation reference. Bars are central 95% intervals in meters; points are medians. These are separate targets, not competing estimates of the same interval.

How to read the plot: The future-year interval includes an additional source of variation. The reference answers a conditional question. Its tighter interval is not evidence that zeroing annual effects produces a more precise forecast of a realized year.

4 Use a model that can predict future responses

The original worked fit contains a fixed factor(year) response effect. It cannot supply a coefficient for an unseen year. Predicting a future scale does not solve that separate response-design limitation.

The next stored fit uses annual response random intercepts as well as annual scale random intercepts. These are separate model components:

forecast_fit <- multiScale_bayes(
  count ~ habitat + (1 | site) + (1 | year),
  annual$inputs, annual$data, row_id = "site_year", family = "nbinom2",
  scales = list(habitat = ~1 + (1 | year)),
  priors = known_fit$specification$priors,
  method = "sample", chains = 4, iter = 8000, warmup = 4000,
  seed = 630127, control = list(adapt_delta = .99, max_treedepth = 12))
Component Role in a future-year prediction
habitat = ~1 + (1 | year) Allows the future landscape scale to differ from the population log scale
Response (1 | year) Allows the future response baseline to differ from the population intercept
Response (1 | site) Reuses a fitted site effect or samples an unsurveyed site’s effect
NB2 observation model Adds count variation around the expected count when simulating observations

Annual response and scale effects are independent conditional on their population parameters in this model. Sharing a grouping label does not fit a correlation between those effects. These effects are exchangeable across years, without temporal autocorrelation or a directional forecast. You can include a numeric year trend when scientifically justified, but prediction then extrapolates that fitted trend. Random slopes, correlated response/scale effects, and temporal dependence are not implemented by this interface.

example <- readRDS(system.file("extdata", "bayesian-prediction-example.rds",
  package = "multiScaleR"))
forecast_fit <- example$fit
knitr::kable(example$fit_info[c("seed", "passed", "derived_max_rhat",
  "minimum_bulk_ess", "minimum_tail_ess", "divergences")], digits = 3,
  col.names = c("Seed", "Passed", "Max Rhat", "Min bulk ESS", "Min tail ESS", "Divergences"))
Seed Passed Max Rhat Min bulk ESS Min tail ESS Divergences

630128 FALSE 1.001 4347.212 4484.146 2

How to read the fit information: The recorded diagnostics describe the original full chains before storing a compact set of complete joint parameter vectors. The designated teaching fit, seed 630127, passed the unchanged raw and derived-scale gates. The independent repeat, seed 630128, had two divergences and remains rejected even though its Rhat and effective sample sizes looked satisfactory. No sampler settings or thresholds were changed to remove that rejection. Inspect every diagnostic, not Rhat alone. This is a worked dataset, not a calibration study. The compact fit has no full sampler for trace plots or a new chain-level convergence audit. For your own fit, inspect summary(forecast_fit)$diagnostics and diagnose_bayes_scales(forecast_fit) before interpreting predictions.

The supplied small bin matrices describe artificial future landscape scenarios derived from the example’s maps. They are not observed 2026/2027 rasters or a forecast of landscape change. Predictions are conditional on these supplied landscapes. They do not propagate map measurement or future-landscape uncertainty. Prepare and compare scientifically plausible landscape scenarios for an actual forecast. Two scenario positions reuse example landscape profiles with new site IDs solely to illustrate unseen-site effects; they are not newly observed locations.

The deterministic scenario builder is supplied at system.file("examples", "bayesian-prediction-data.R", package = "multiScaleR"). Sourcing it defines make_bayesian_prediction_scenario(), which recreates these eight rows and bin matrices without fitting, sampling or writing files.

Use comparable predictor definitions, bin resolution and preparation coverage. A smaller future buffer renormalizes a more truncated kernel. Raster edges and missing cells can matter even when buffers have the same nominal extent. Prediction does not certify that coverage. You can inspect the scale distribution against the future inputs’ reference range and convert each scale to an ideal planar kernel radius:

log_D <- predict_scales(forecast_fit, future_years, new_levels = "sample",
  scale = "log", summary = FALSE, seed = 806)$habitat
radius_per_D <- kernel_dist(prob = .95, dimension = "2d",
  sigma = 1, kernel = forecast_fit$specification$kernel)
knitr::kable(data.frame(year = future_years$year,
  p_D_above_max_D = colMeans(log_D > log(future_inputs$max_D)),
  p_radius95_above_max_D = colMeans(log_D + log(radius_per_D) >
    log(future_inputs$max_D))), digits = 3)
year p_D_above_max_D p_radius95_above_max_D
2026 0.001 0.025
2027 0.000 0.025

How to read this check: The first probability concerns the scale parameter itself. The second concerns the radius containing 95% of weight under the ideal, untruncated planar kernel, which extends beyond one scale parameter. Both retain posterior and new-year uncertainty. They do not measure actual cell coverage near raster edges or gaps. Inspect those separately. If larger neighborhoods are scientifically needed, rebuild comparable fitting and prediction inputs and refit rather than silently changing the predictor’s finite support only at prediction time.

How to read these outputs: All rows have a new annual scale. A response row is also flagged if it includes a new response year or site. Known sites retain their fitted site effects even in future years. The new_groups attribute lists the component, predictor, grouping variable, new level and chosen action. It distinguishes a new annual response effect from a new annual scale effect, although both use year.

Choice Scale component Response component Meaning
Require an explicit decision new_scale_levels = "error" new_response_levels = "error" Default; an unseen included group stops prediction
Predict a new group new_scale_levels = "sample" new_response_levels = "sample" Sample one new effect per group and complete posterior vector
Zero only unseen effects new_scale_levels = "zero" new_response_levels = "zero" Keep known effects, set new deviations to zero
Omit all group effects in a component scale_re.form = NA re.form = NA Set known and new deviations in that component to zero

Zero-deviation predictions retain uncertainty in fixed parameters. They do not integrate over group variation, particularly after nonlinear link functions or kernels. An omitted grouping column is unnecessary only when all effects that require it are omitted and no fixed term also uses it. An unseen fixed-factor level remains an error under every policy.

How to read the output: Count intervals include uncertainty in expected counts and NB2 observation variation. They can be much wider than intervals for expected counts. The final interval concerns the sum over all supplied surveys. It retains shared effects, including the same new year at different sites and the same new site across years. Summing separate interval endpoints would not calculate an interval for that total.

Within one call, observations in the same new group share its sampled effect for a complete predictive dataset. Different new groups are independent conditional on their population parameters, whose joint posterior dependence is preserved. Separate calls do not define a shared realization across components or batches. Supply all rows together when their joint prediction matters. Reusing a seed across predict_scales(), predict() and simulate() does not promise matching latent effects across those different algorithms.

simulate() returns rows by replicates. predict(summary = FALSE) instead returns posterior vectors by rows, and predict_scales(summary = FALSE) returns a named list of such matrices, one per landscape predictor. These orientations matter when computing joint contrasts or totals.

6.1 Binary and grouped-binomial observations

For a binary fit, new observations default to one trial. For a cbind(successes, failures) fit, supply new trial totals explicitly:

# binomial_fit is an existing fit to grouped successes/failures.
probability <- predict(binomial_fit, newdata = new_surveys,
  kernel_inputs = future_inputs, row_id = "site_year",
  new_scale_levels = "sample", new_response_levels = "sample", seed = 804)
successes <- simulate(binomial_fit, nsim = 1000, seed = 805,
  newdata = new_surveys, kernel_inputs = future_inputs, row_id = "site_year",
  trials = rep(10L, nrow(new_surveys)),
  new_scale_levels = "sample", new_response_levels = "sample")

probability summarizes success probabilities, regardless of the number of trials. successes contains integer counts out of ten. Use the actual future trial totals; future response columns are ignored and never determine them.

7 Check predictions before using them

Prediction propagates the fitted posterior; it does not repair failed sampling, inaccurate approximation, or a misspecified observation model. All supported inference methods use their retained complete joint vectors. Importance-corrected fits use their stored joint resamples here, so prediction has the finite precision of those resamples rather than a fresh weighted integration. More predictive replicates reduce simulation noise conditional on the retained posterior but do not add posterior effective sample size.

Scale summaries compute each distinct active covariate/group combination once. A large stack sharing two annual scales therefore needs only two scale columns internally when summary = TRUE. Full scale draws and response predictions still allocate posterior vectors by observation rows. For individual rowwise intervals you can process smaller batches and retain only their summaries. Independently simulated batches do not define a coherent joint forecast for shared groups or totals. Use one batch for those targets.

Check held-out responses at the level you intend to predict. Holding out a visit at a known site and year is easier than holding out an entire year or site. Evaluate interval coverage, predictive scores and scientifically relevant summaries within those separate tasks. Repeated sites or years are not independent validation replicates. Good computational diagnostics and one successful teaching example do not establish predictive calibration.

Prediction fails on unmatched IDs, new fixed-factor levels, unavailable formula transforms, incompatible kernel inputs, and nonfinite results. It does not silently clip scales to input limits or replace problematic draws. Use deterministic formula transforms. Standard base/stats transforms and namespace-qualified transforms must remain available; custom functions saved only in a fitting workspace are not serialized with the model. scale() and poly() retain their fitting definitions for prediction. An expression such as I(x - mean(x)) instead computes its mean on the supplied new batch by ordinary R formula rules. Use precomputed training constants when you need to retain that center.

8 Quick reference

  new_levels = "sample", seed = 801)
